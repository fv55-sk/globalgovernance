Part 1: Defining the Global Economy & Economic Globalization

Exact Slide & Audio Definitions of Global Economy

Slide Definition 1 "It is the economy of all humans of the world, considered as the international exchange of goods and services that is expressed in monetary units of account."[cite: 1]
Slide Definition 2 "The economy of our planet. All the economies added up. Everything we buy, everything we sell, everything we make, everything we own, all imports, all exports."[cite: 1]
Slide Definition 3 "The global economy is the world economy or worldwide economy. It is all the economies of the world (the economy of every country) which we consider together as one economic system."[cite: 1]
Slide Definition 4 "Put simply; the GLOBAL ECONOMY is one giant entity."[cite: 1]
Slide Definition 5 "It is also the system of trade and industry across the world that has emerged due to globalization. In other words, the way in which countries' economies have been developing to operate collectively as one system."[cite: 1]

Four Dimensions of Global Economy (Slide Presentation)

  • Globalization of trade of goods and services: Cross-border trade that is no longer confined to individual nations or regions[cite: 1].
  • The globalization of financial and capital markets: International financial institutions operating and moving financial capital worldwide[cite: 1].
  • The globalization of technology and communication: Rapid worldwide access and sharing of technological tools and communications[cite: 1].
  • The globalization of production: Moving goods manufacturing from the First World (Europe, North America) into developing nations in Asia, Africa, and Third World countries[cite: 1].

Internationalization vs. Economic Globalization

Internationalization

Verbatim Slide Meaning "About the extension of economic activities of nation states across borders."[cite: 1]

Characterized as a purely quantitative change (expansion in distance and volume of trade across national frontiers)[cite: 1].

Economic Globalization

Verbatim Slide Meaning "Functional integration between internationally dispersed activities. That is, a qualitative transformation than just quantitative change."[cite: 1]

A deep transformation altering consumer taste, culture, and systemic functional interdependence across societies[cite: 1].

Historical Background (Archaic Globalization)

  • The Silk Road: The existence of the world system in which we live stretches back at least 5,000 years, connecting Asia, Africa, and Europe[cite: 1].
  • Age of Discovery: The discovery of America by Christopher Columbus (1492) and the direct sea route to India by Vasco da Gama[cite: 1].
  • British Industrialization & Napoleonic Wars: Technological advances and factory organization spreading from Britain across Continental Europe and North America[cite: 1].
  • Founding of First MNCs: The British East India Company and Dutch East India Company (which drove the colonization of countries like Malaysia)[cite: 1].

Part 1: The Great Depression (1929–1939)

Exact Slide & Audio Meaning

Verbatim Definition "A depression is a sustained, a long-term downturn in economic activity in on or more economies. Considered by some economists, a rare, and extreme form of recession."[cite: 1]

The worst economic downturn in the history of the industrialized world, lasting from 1929 to 1939, beginning in the United States and triggering global economic collapse[cite: 1].

Causes of the Great Depression (Slide by Slide)

  • Over Production:
    • High demand and high prices for agricultural products during World War I[cite: 1].
    • Demand and prices fell sharply after the war[cite: 1].
    • Farmers could not pay back their bank loans[cite: 1].
  • Decrease in Consumer Spending:
    • Rising prices and low wages[cite: 1].
    • Overbuying on credit; large debts had to be paid off[cite: 1].
    • Uneven distribution of income — rich got richer and poor got poorer — many Americans could not afford to buy goods produced[cite: 1].
  • Over Speculation:
    • Stocks bought on the hope of quick profit[cite: 1].
    • Buying on Margin: Paying a small % of stock's price and borrowing the rest through broker loans[cite: 1].
    • High tariffs harmed world trade[cite: 1].

"The Bubble Bursts" & Domino Effect (Slide Statistics)

  • Bank Failures: Banks failed because people withdrew funds in panicked bank runs[cite: 1]. Banks had invested heavily in the stock market and could not repay loans[cite: 1].
  • 1933 Failure Stat: By 1933, 11,000 of nation's 25,000 banks had failed[cite: 1]. Accounts were not insured, causing millions to lose their lifetime savings[cite: 1].
  • The Domino Effect: People lose jobs → businesses close down or lay off → more people lose jobs[cite: 1].
  • Unemployment Scale: 80,000 seat football stadium metric → continued for 162 weeks → 13 million unemployed in the US alone[cite: 1].
  • World War II (1939–1945): A global war lasting from 1939 to 1945 that worsened economic hardships before postwar recovery was organized[cite: 1, 2].

Part 2: The Bretton Woods Conference (1944)

Formal Title: Formally known as the United Nations Monetary and Financial Conference held in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, USA (July 1–22, 1944), bringing together 730 delegates from all 44 Allied nations to regulate the international monetary and financial order after WWII[cite: 2].

Two Conflicting Views on the Role of the IMF

Harry Dexter White (USA)

Slide Verbatim "He foresaw an IMF that functioned more like a bank, making sure that borrowing states could repay their debts on time."[cite: 2]

John Maynard Keynes (UK)

Slide Verbatim "He imagined that the IMF would be a cooperative fund upon which member states could draw to maintain economic activity and employment through periodic crises."[cite: 2]

Three Multilateral Postwar Institutions Created

  1. International Monetary Fund (IMF):
    • Articles of Agreement conceived in July 1944; formally came into existence on 27 December 1945 after 29 countries ratified the Articles of Agreement; began financial operations on March 1, 1947[cite: 2].
    • First Country to Borrow: On May 8, 1947, France became the first country to borrow from the IMF[cite: 2].
    • Fundamental Missions:
      1. Economic Surveillance: Oversees the international monetary system and monitors the economic and financial policies of its member countries[cite: 2].
      2. Lending: Provides loans to member countries experiencing actual or potential balance of payments problems to help them rebuild their international reserves[cite: 2].
      3. Capacity Development: Modernizes economic policies and institutions and trains people[cite: 2].
    • IMF Resources (Member Quotas): Member Quotas are the primary source of IMF financial resources[cite: 2]. A country's quota determines its maximum financial commitment to the IMF, its voting power, and its access to IMF financing[cite: 2].
  2. The World Bank:
    • Designed to finance projects that enhance economic development of member states and provides technical assistance and policy advice[cite: 2]. Promotes sustainable development to narrow the income gap between rich and poor countries[cite: 2].
    • Composed of 5 specialized bodies:
      1. IBRD: International Bank for Reconstruction and Development[cite: 2]
      2. IDA: International Development Association[cite: 2]
      3. IFC: International Finance Corporation[cite: 2]
      4. MIGA: Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency[cite: 2]
      5. ICSID: International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes[cite: 2]
  3. General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT):
    • An interim agreement signed in 1947 that served as a forum for trade negotiations[cite: 2].
    • Evolved in 1994 into the formal World Trade Organization (WTO)[cite: 2].

Part 2: Full Detailed Study of Neo-liberalism vs. Neo-mercantilism

1. Neo-liberalism (Free Market Policies)

Neo-liberalism is the free market policy framework that champions economic freedom, open borders, global capital flows, and the reduction of state interference in trade and commerce[cite: 2].

A. Liberalization

Slide 52 Verbatim Definition "Liberalization is the reduction of restrictions or barriers on the free exchange of goods between nations."[cite: 2]

Detailed Meaning According to the Lecturer:

  • The goal is to eliminate or significantly reduce tariffs, duties, and barriers so goods flow across countries freely[cite: 2].
  • The speaker notes that while the textbook definition specifies the reduction of restrictions or barriers, some countries go so far as the complete elimination of barriers (zero tariffs) under Free Trade Agreements[cite: 2].
  • Example Discussed in Audio: If the Philippine government imposes a 50% tariff on an imported luxury car valued at $1,000,000 USD (paying hundreds of millions of pesos in customs taxes), that is a protectionist barrier[cite: 2]. Under trade liberalization, that tariff rate is reduced (e.g., down to 30%, 20%, or completely down to 0%) to encourage cross-border trade[cite: 2].

B. Deregulation

Slide 53 Verbatim Definition "Deregulation is the reduction and elimination of government power in a particular industry, usually enacted to create more competition within the industry."[cite: 2]

Detailed Meaning According to the Lecturer:

  • Under strict government regulation, the state sets prices, restricts market entry, and controls who can operate[cite: 2].
  • Deregulation removes these governmental constraints, permitting multiple private players to enter, which drives competitive pricing and expands consumer choices[cite: 2].
  • Basic industries discussed: telecommunications, energy/power, and water utilities[cite: 2].

C. Privatization

Slide 55 Verbatim Definition "Privatization is the transfer of ownership, property or business from the government to the private sector."[cite: 2]

Detailed Meaning According to the Lecturer:

  • When industries or companies owned, operated, and controlled by the state are sold off and transferred into the hands of private corporations or investors[cite: 2].

D. The Philippine Historical Case for Neo-Liberalism

The lecturer explicitly grounds the shift to neo-liberal policies in modern Philippine economic history:

  • President Corazon Aquino (1986): Following the end of the Marcos administration in 1986, the Aquino government took over basic industries and utilities that were formerly controlled by the state[cite: 2].
  • The government enacted policies of deregulation and privatization, selling off state-owned corporations and basic utility assets (such as water, power, and telecommunications) to private enterprises to stimulate competition and economic recovery[cite: 2].

2. Neo-mercantilism (Interventionist / Protectionist Policies)

Neo-mercantilism is an economic philosophy that relies on state intervention to protect domestic industries, limit imports, and accumulate national wealth through trade surpluses[cite: 2].

Comprehensive Breakdown of Slide 51 Points & Lecturer Meaning:

1. Tariff and Non-Tariff Barrier Imposition:

  • Tariffs: Taxes or customs duties placed directly on imported goods to make foreign products more expensive than domestically produced goods[cite: 2].
  • Non-Tariff Barriers: Administrative rules, licenses, and documentation requirements deliberately constructed to discourage, slow down, or block foreign imports[cite: 2].

2. Tight State Regulation:

  • The national government directly dictates, controls, and oversees the operation of domestic markets, establishing price ceilings, price floors, and strict business licensing requirements[cite: 2].

3. Subsidies:

  • Direct financial assistance or tax incentives given by the government to domestic producers, farmers, or manufacturers so they can lower their production costs and sell their products cheaply without being driven out by foreign competition[cite: 2].

4. Import Quotas:

  • Strict legal limits established by the state regarding the exact volume or quantity of a specific foreign good that is permitted to enter the country over a given time period[cite: 2].

5. Health and Environment Standardization:

  • Imposing exceptionally high, rigorous health, safety, and environmental standards that foreign imports must satisfy before entering the domestic market[cite: 2]. The lecturer notes these are often weaponized as protectionist non-tariff barriers to block foreign competitors from entering[cite: 2].

6. Import Substitution:

  • A core protectionist strategy where a country actively manufactures goods domestically to replace products it previously had to import from abroad, building national industrial self-sufficiency and preventing national wealth from flowing out[cite: 2].

The Philippine Historical Case for Protectionism:

  • President Ferdinand Marcos Sr. (Martial Law Era): Used by the lecturer as the primary example of an interventionist, protectionist policy structure[cite: 2].
  • Basic industries and public utilities were held under strict, centralized state ownership, management, and control—specifically:
    • Telecommunications: PLDT[cite: 2]
    • Electricity & Power Generation: National Power Corporation (NPC)[cite: 2]
    • Water Distribution: State-controlled water utility administration[cite: 2]
  • The government exercised monopoly control over these critical sectors to prevent foreign domination and manage the domestic economy directly from the state level[cite: 2].
